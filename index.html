A curb inspection of the organizations in the AI-risk debate · Level 1, from the public record

A frontier lab’s attack surface includes everyone it lets test the model.

Before a frontier model ships, its developer hands early access to outside organizations: evaluation nonprofits, red-team firms, biosecurity specialists, government institutes. The labs name them in their own system cards. From the moment the access is granted, each of those organizations is part of the lab’s perimeter, and nobody publishes a measure of it. This map is that measure, taken from the curb.

The proof that the soft edge is the real edge is already on the record. In July 2026 a three-person research team got into OpenAI’s GitHub organization and employee accounts through an image-upload flaw on its community forum, a peripheral website nobody thinks of as the vault (TechCrunch, 18 September 2026). Attackers do not go through the front of the building. They go through whatever is attached to it.

65 organizations, each here because of a document it signed or a lab signed about it: the evaluators the labs entrusted with unreleased models, the labs that pledged at Seoul to invest in cybersecurity for exactly those models, the organizations warning the public about AI risk, the government institutes charged with measuring it, and the AI developers and security-ratings firms that signed August’s call for every organization to “fix the highest-risk weaknesses.” Each is read against a published code, the Frontier Perimeter Security Standard, in two tiers: The Floor, the minimum any of them should clear, and Standard Practice above it. Every control is pass, below code, advisable, or not observable. Never a score.

This is a mirror, not a verdict. The observable Floor costs close to nothing to meet, which is why it is the same for a trillion-dollar lab and a ten-person nonprofit. Nothing was supplied by any organization and nothing was tested against its systems. Any organization on the map can work the Level 2 self-assessment, or ask for a re-measure.

65 of 65 organizations carry a linked inclusion source you can open and check, with the sentence that put them here quoted on their profile.

No list was bought or hand-picked. Each ring comes from one public document: the Frontier AI Safety Commitments signed at the AI Seoul Summit; the external-testing sections of Anthropic, OpenAI and Google DeepMind system cards from 2024 to 2026; the organizations’ own published warnings and lab ratings; the membership of the international network of AI safety institutes; and the 666 signatories of the Collective Cyber Defense letter, from which only the AI developers and the security-ratings firms are drawn. Every inclusion sentence was read back from its source. Where the evidence was thin (a heading without a sentence, a co-authored paper, a one-person project that has stopped) the organization was held back, not included on a generous reading; the Method lists every one and why.

What this is: a Level 1 curb inspection of the organizations in the AI-risk debate, against a published code, with every reading dated, sourced and bounded on the Method page. What it is not: a security rating, a ranking, a penetration test or a finding about any organization’s internal controls. The controls that protect model weights from a capable attacker sit behind Level 2 and beyond it; a clean curb says nothing about them, and a mark below the Floor says only what it says. Any organization can ask for a re-measure. Inclusion is neither an endorsement nor an accusation. By Pete Depok (Deep Hawks).
